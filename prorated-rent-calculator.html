How to Calculate Prorated Rent: The Complete 2026 Landlord & Tenant Guide

In standard residential and commercial leasing, a tenancy rarely starts precisely on the first day of the calendar month or terminates cleanly on the last. When a tenant moves in mid-month or vacates prior to the 30th or 31st, charging a full month's rent is unfair to the tenant, while granting the partial month free results in unrecovered property carrying costs for the landlord.

Prorated rent solves this friction by adjusting the rental charge proportionally to reflect only the specific days the tenant maintains physical possession, access, or custody of the property.

Universal Calendar-Day Prorated Rent Formula
$$\text{Prorated Rent} = \left( \frac{\text{Monthly Rent}}{\text{Total Days in That Specific Month}} \right) \times \text{Days Occupied}$$

The 4 Industry Standard Proration Methods Explained

While dividing the rent seems straightforward, real estate contracts across the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada utilize four distinct mathematical conventions. The specific method enforced depends on state law and the explicit wording in your lease agreement:

  1. 1. Exact Calendar Days Method (Most Common & Fair): The monthly rent is divided by the actual number of calendar days in that specific month (28 or 29 days in February, 30 in April/June/September/November, and 31 in January/March/May/July/August/October/December). This method guarantees that a tenant pays precisely their fair share based on that month's length.
  2. 2. Banker's 30-Day Rule: Assumes every month in the year has exactly 30 days ($30 \times 12 = 360\text{ days}$). Under this clause, the daily rate is always $\text{Monthly Rent} \div 30$. In a 31-day month like October, this method results in a slightly higher daily rate than the calendar method.
  3. 3. Annual 365-Day Method (Yearly Lease Standard): Multiplies the monthly rent by 12 to establish the annual contract value, then divides by 365 days (or 366 in a leap year): $$\text{Daily Rate} = \frac{\text{Monthly Rent} \times 12}{365}$$ This method produces an unvarying, consistent daily rent throughout the entire year and is favored by corporate multifamily property management companies.
  4. 4. Banking Year (360-Day Method): Frequently utilized in commercial real estate leases and bank loan amortizations, dividing the annual rent by 360 days: $$\text{Daily Rate} = \frac{\text{Monthly Rent} \times 12}{360}$$

Real-World Example: Moving In on March 15th

Consider a tenant renting an apartment for $1,800/month with a move-in date of March 15th. March has 31 calendar days. The tenant occupies the home from March 15 through March 31 inclusive, totaling 17 days.

  • Exact Calendar Method (31 days): $(\$1,800 \div 31) \times 17 = \$58.0645 \times 17 = \mathbf{\$987.10}$
  • 30-Day Banker Rule: $(\$1,800 \div 30) \times 17 = \$60.00 \times 17 = \mathbf{\$1,020.00}$
  • Annual 365-Day Method: $(\$21,600 \div 365) \times 17 = \$59.178 \times 17 = \mathbf{\$1,006.03}$

As demonstrated, the tenant saves $32.90 using the Exact Calendar Days method compared to the 30-Day rule during a 31-day month.

First-Month vs. Second-Month Proration Strategy

A frequent dilemma for landlords is whether to charge prorated rent immediately upon key handover, or collect a full month's rent upfront and apply the proration discount to the second month:

  • Immediate First-Month Proration: The tenant pays only the partial rent plus the security deposit at lease signing. On the 1st of the subsequent month, their regular full recurring payment begins. This reduces upfront move-in cash strain on the tenant.
  • Second-Month Proration (Corporate Policy): The tenant pays a full month's rent upfront plus the security deposit at signing. On the 1st of the second month, they pay only the prorated balance. This policy protects landlords against immediate tenant defaults or fraudulent move-ins where tenants vacate after paying only a few days of rent.

Frequently Asked Questions About Prorated Rent

What is prorated rent and how does it work? +
Prorated rent is the proportionate amount of rent charged when a tenant occupies a rental home for only part of a normal billing month (such as moving in on the 12th or moving out on the 18th). The landlord calculates the daily cost of the property and multiplies it strictly by the number of days the tenant holds physical possession.
Does the day of move-in count as a billable day? +
Yes. Under standard US real estate law and lease agreements, the move-in day is counted as an occupied day because the tenant has legal right, access, keys, and physical possession of the property from that date forward.
Are landlords legally required to prorate rent? +
There is no overarching federal statute requiring landlords to prorate rent. However, state common law, landlord-tenant acts (such as California Civil Code § 1946.1, Texas Property Code § 92, and New York Real Property Law), and standard contract interpretation prevent landlords from charging full monthly rent when they do not deliver possession for the full month, as doing so constitutes unjust enrichment.
How does a leap year affect prorated rent in February? +
During a leap year, February contains 29 days instead of 28. Under the Exact Calendar Days method, daily rent is calculated by dividing monthly rent by 29 instead of 28, which slightly reduces the daily rate and saves the tenant money. Under the 30-day banker rule, February is treated as 30 days regardless of leap year status.
Can a tenant demand prorated rent if they move out early voluntarily? +
No. If a tenant on a standard fixed-term lease decides to vacate before the end of the month without statutory justification or proper written notice required by their lease (usually 30 or 60 days), the landlord is generally not obligated to refund or prorate rent for the unused days of that month.
Which proration method is best for tenants? +
For 31-day months (January, March, May, July, August, October, December), the Exact Calendar Days method is best for tenants because dividing by 31 yields the lowest daily rate. For February (28 days), the 30-Day Banker Rule is best for tenants because dividing by 30 produces a lower daily rate than dividing by 28.
