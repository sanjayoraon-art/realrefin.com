Understanding the Maximum Allowable Offer (MAO) in Real Estate

In real estate wholesaling and fix-and-flip investing, the Maximum Allowable Offer (MAO) represents the supreme mathematical threshold for deal acquisition. It is the highest purchase price an investor can offer a seller while ensuring adequate downside capital protection, absorption of unforeseen renovation cost overruns, and realization of target net return on investment (ROI).

Submitting offers based on subjective emotion or unverified comparable sales represents the primary reason novice investors suffer capital impairment. Institutional flippers and high-volume wholesale operators engineer acquisitions backward from the After Repair Value (ARV) down to the dollar.

The Classic Real Estate 70% Rule Formula
$$\text{MAO} = (\text{ARV} \times 0.70) - \text{Estimated Repair Costs} - \text{Wholesale Fee}$$

Deconstructing the 70% Rule: Where Does the Other 30% Go?

A frequent misconception among beginning real estate practitioners is that the 70% rule yields a 30% net profit margin for the investor. In institutional reality, that 30% equity allocation accommodates three distinct expense buckets:

  • Holding & Carrying Costs (Approx. 4%–6%): Property taxes, hazard insurance, utility maintenance, municipal permit fees, and debt service (interest charges and origination points paid to hard money lenders) during the renovation and marketing cycles.
  • Acquisition & Disposition Transaction Costs (Approx. 8%–10%): Title insurance, escrow settlement fees, transfer taxes, attorney fees, staging expenses, and real estate brokerage commissions (typically 5% to 6% paid on the final disposition sale).
  • Net Investor Profit Margin (Approx. 14%–18%): The true bottom-line compensation required by the fix-and-flipper to justify the operational risk and capital outlay of the repositioning project.

💡 Pro Wholesaler Nuance

If you are wholesaling rather than executing the physical renovation yourself, you must always subtract your desired contract assignment fee from the standard flipper's MAO. Failing to deduct your assignment fee means your cash buyer's profit margin will be compressed, drastically reducing their likelihood of funding the contract.

How to Accurately Estimate After Repair Value (ARV)

Your MAO calculation is only as reliable as your baseline ARV appraisal. To accurately establish the After Repair Value without an appraisal report, use the following strict underwriting criteria:

  1. Geographic Proximity: Select strictly comparable properties ("comps") located within 0.5 miles in urban/suburban subdivisions, never crossing major commercial thoroughfares, railroad tracks, or school district boundaries.
  2. Recency: Prioritize closed transactions recorded within the preceding 90 days. In fluctuating interest rate climates, comps older than 180 days are obsolete.
  3. Property Characteristics: Ensure comps share similar gross living area (GLA) within $\pm 15\%$, comparable bedroom and bathroom counts, similar architectural style, and equivalent basement foundations.
  4. Renovation Quality: Comps must reflect fully renovated, turn-key retail condition—including updated mechanicals, modern quartz or granite countertops, remodeled bathrooms, and premium flooring.

Adjusting the Rule: When to Use 65%, 75%, or 80%

While the 70% rule remains the universal real estate baseline, local market microclimates dictate calibration:

  • The 65% Rule (Buyer's Market / High Interest Rates): When borrowing costs exceed 10% and inventory sits on market beyond 60 days, expanding the discount buffer to 65% shields the investor against prolonged holding carry and price depreciation.
  • The 75% Rule (Balanced Metropolitan Markets): Common in prime metropolitan submarkets with stable days-on-market metrics and high consumer demand for renovated entry-level housing.
  • The 80% Rule (Hyper-Competitive / High-Value Markets): In premier urban coastal submarkets (e.g., Southern California, Northern New Jersey, South Florida) with median home prices exceeding $750,000, investors frequently bid at 80% because fixed closing and holding expenses represent a smaller percentage of overall transaction proceeds.

Frequently Asked Questions About MAO & The 70% Rule

What is the Maximum Allowable Offer (MAO) in real estate? +
The Maximum Allowable Offer (MAO) is the absolute highest dollar amount an investor or wholesaler can offer a property seller while still ensuring target profit margins and covering all acquisition, renovation, holding, financing, and resale costs. Offering above the MAO drastically reduces profit and risks capital loss.
What is the 70% Rule in real estate investing? +
The 70% Rule is a widely adopted real estate rule of thumb stating that an investor should pay no more than 70% of the property's After Repair Value (ARV), minus the estimated cost of repairs. The mathematical formula is: MAO = (ARV × 0.70) − Estimated Repair Costs. The remaining 30% margin accounts for closing costs, holding expenses, financing costs, and the investor's net profit.
How do wholesalers calculate MAO differently from flippers? +
Wholesalers must subtract their desired wholesale assignment fee from the standard flipper MAO. The wholesale MAO formula is: Wholesale MAO = (ARV × 0.70) − Repair Costs − Wholesale Fee. This guarantees that when the wholesaler assigns the contract to an end cash buyer at the flipper's MAO, the end investor still achieves their full 15% to 20% profit margin.
Should you ever adjust the 70% Rule to 75% or 80%? +
Yes. In high-demand, low-inventory hot markets with rapid days on market (DOM) and predictable appreciation, investors frequently increase their benchmark to 75% or 80% of ARV. Conversely, in higher-interest-rate environments, slow buyer markets, or for heavy structural gut renovations, conservative investors reduce the formula to 60% or 65% to account for extended holding durations and market volatility.
How do you accurately estimate After Repair Value (ARV)? +
ARV is determined by analyzing 3 to 5 recent comparable sales ('comps') within a 0.5 to 1-mile radius that sold in the last 90 to 180 days. Comps must match the subject property in square footage (±15%), bedroom/bathroom count, year built, and finish level after full modern renovation.
What hidden costs are most often missed when calculating MAO? +
The most commonly overlooked expenses include hard money origination points and monthly interest carry costs, municipal building permit delays, transfer taxes, title insurance, contractor contingency reserves (10%-15%), and seller concessions on the final disposition sale.
