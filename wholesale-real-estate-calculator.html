Comprehensive Guide: Real Estate Wholesaling & Offer Calculation

In real estate investing, wholesaling is the foundational business model that bridges the gap between distressed, motivated property owners and cash investors (fix & flippers or landlords). A wholesaler locates off-market properties at a substantial discount, places them under a legally binding purchase contract, and either assigns the contract or double-closes with an end-buyer for a lucrative assignment fee.

Without a precise **wholesale real estate calculator**, wholesalers risk putting properties under contract at prices too high for cash buyers to purchase—wasting marketing dollars, damaging investor relationships, and risking earnest money deposits.

What is the Wholesale Price in Real Estate?

The Wholesale Price is the deeply discounted contract price negotiated with the motivated seller. It must be sufficiently low to allow the end-buyer cash investor to purchase the property, complete all repairs, pay carrying and closing costs, and earn an attractive return on investment (ROI)—while leaving a $10,000 to $25,000+ assignment fee for the wholesaler.

How to Calculate Wholesale Price in Real Estate (The Formula)

Wholesale offer underwriting follows a strict mathematical reverse-engineering process:

The Core Wholesaling Formula

$$\text{End-Buyer Maximum Purchase Price} = (\text{ARV} \times \text{Buyer Rule \%}) - \text{Estimated Rehab Cost}$$ $$\text{Wholesale Maximum Allowable Offer (MAO)} = \text{End-Buyer Purchase Price} - \text{Wholesaler Assignment Fee}$$

Step-by-Step Numerical Example:

  1. Determine ARV: Comparable sales show similar renovated homes selling for $375,000.
  2. Estimate Rehab Costs: Property inspection indicates $65,000 in necessary renovation costs.
  3. Apply the 70% Rule: $\$375,000 \times 0.70 = \$262,500$.
  4. Subtract Renovation: $\$262,500 - \$65,000 = \$197,500$ (The maximum cash price your end buyer will pay).
  5. Deduct Your Assignment Fee: If your target wholesale profit is $15,000: $$\text{MAO to Seller} = \$197,500 - \$15,000 = \$182,500$$

By offering the seller $182,500 or less, you have guaranteed your $15,000 assignment spread while packaging an irresistible 70% rule deal for your cash buyers list.

Assignment of Contract vs. Double Closing (Simultaneous Close)

When structuring wholesale deals, wholesalers must choose between two closing mechanisms based on the size of the assignment fee:

Wholesale Strategy How It Works Ideal Assignment Spread Pros & Cons
Assignment of Contract Wholesaler assigns equitable interest in purchase agreement to cash buyer for an assignment fee. $5,000 to $20,000 Pros: Zero capital required, buyer pays closing fees.
Cons: Fee is disclosed to seller on HUD-1.
Double Closing (Simultaneous) Two separate closings (A-to-B with seller, then B-to-C with end buyer) using transactional funding. $25,000 to $60,000+ Pros: Complete fee confidentiality.
Cons: Requires 1%-2% transactional funding fee.

When contracting off-market residential deals directly with motivated sellers, wholesalers typically put down an earnest money deposit (EMD) between $100 and $1,000. When assigning the contract to the end cash buyer, the wholesaler requires a larger, non-refundable deposit (typically $2,500 to $5,000) to ensure the buyer performs at closing.
Wholesalers standardly include a 10 to 14-day inspection contingency clause (e.g., 'Purchase contract is contingent upon buyer's inspection and partner/contractor approval'). This period allows the wholesaler to bring prospective cash buyers and contractors to tour the property without risking legal liability or forfeiture of earnest money if a buyer is not found.

The 70% rule is the historic standard for flippers in moderate or balanced housing markets. However, in competitive, high-demand metropolitan markets (such as Phoenix, Dallas, or Atlanta) or for light cosmetic renovations, cash buyers frequently underwrite at 75% or even 80% of ARV. Wholesalers adjust their buy box rule percentage to match local investor demand.
Yes, wholesaling real estate is legal across the United States. Wholesalers are legally marketing and assigning their contractual equitable interest in a purchase agreement, rather than acting as unlicensed real estate brokers representing third parties. However, states like Illinois, Oklahoma, and Texas have enacted specific disclosure guidelines requiring wholesalers to clearly state that they are selling contract rights and hold equitable title.
