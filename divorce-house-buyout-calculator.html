How is House Buyout Calculated in a Divorce?

A marital home equity buyout occurs when one spouse elects to retain sole ownership of the marital residence and compensates the departing spouse for their legally earned share of the home's accrued equity. If you are researching how is house buyout calculated in a divorce, the baseline legal mathematical formula is:

⚖️ Standard Divorce House Buyout Formula

Net Buyout Amount = [(Fair Market Appraised Value - Total Mortgages & Liens - Deductions) × Departing Spouse Share %] - Asset Offsets

While the formula appears mathematically straightforward, negotiations frequently involve disputes over property appraisals, theoretical real estate broker fees, pre-marital financial contributions, and qualifying for an equity buyout refinance on a single income.

How to Calculate House Buyout in Divorce: The 5-Step Process

To ensure fair financial resolution and protect both spouses from post-decree mortgage liability, courts and certified divorce financial analysts (CDFAs) follow this structured 5-step underwriting process:

Step 1: Obtain a Joint Certified Appraisal

Do not rely on online automated valuation models (AVMs) such as Zillow or Redfin. Spouses should mutually agree on an independent, licensed real estate appraiser to conduct an on-site professional appraisal. This establishes the defensible Fair Market Value (FMV) of the home.

Step 2: Calculate Gross Marital Home Equity

Contact your mortgage loan servicer to request an official 30-day mortgage payoff statement. Add together your primary mortgage balance, any second mortgage, and any outstanding Home Equity Line of Credit (HELOC). Subtract this cumulative debt from the appraised value:

  • Appraised Fair Market Value: $550,000
  • Less Primary Mortgage Balance: -$320,000
  • Gross Marital Equity: $230,000

Step 3: Negotiate Notional Selling Costs & Separate Property Credits

One of the most intensely debated topics in divorce law is notional selling costs. If the couple were to sell the home on the open market, they would incur approximately 6% to 8% in realtor commissions, title insurance, and municipal transfer taxes. Many divorce attorneys argue that the retaining spouse should receive a 6% credit against the home's value, because they will shoulder 100% of those selling fees alone when they eventually sell the property in the future. Check with your family law attorney to see if your jurisdiction allows notional cost deductions.

Step 4: Factor Pre-Marital Down Payment Contributions

In community property jurisdictions (such as California Family Code § 2640), if one spouse utilized separate, pre-marital funds or an inheritance to pay the original down payment or capital renovations, that spouse is entitled to direct reimbursement off the top of the gross equity before dividing marital appreciation.

Step 5: Determine How the Buyout Will Be Funded

The spouse keeping the home must deliver the agreed cash payout to the departing spouse and legally release them from the existing promissory note. This is typically executed via:

  1. Equity Buyout Refinance: The retaining spouse refinances the property under their sole name, rolling the existing mortgage balance plus the buyout sum into a single new loan.
  2. Retirement Account Trade / QDRO: Instead of taking out a new loan, the staying spouse trades an equivalent value of marital 401(k), IRA, or pension assets using a Qualified Domestic Relations Order (QDRO).
  3. Mortgage Assumption: If the current mortgage is an FHA or VA loan with a low 3% interest rate, the retaining spouse can apply for an assumption with a formal release of liability, paying the equity buyout from separate savings.

Financing an Equity Buyout: Fannie Mae Special Rules

Homeowners often fear that buying out an ex-spouse requires an expensive cash-out refinance. Standard cash-out refinances carry strict 80% Loan-to-Value (LTV) limits and higher interest rate pricing adjustments.

However, under Fannie Mae Selling Guide Section B2-1.3-02 and Freddie Mac guidelines, an equity buyout refinance is classified as a Rate-and-Term Limited Cash-Out Refinance, provided that:

  • The divorce settlement agreement or court order explicitly awards the property to the remaining spouse and dictates the specific buyout sum.
  • The proceeds of the new loan are paid directly to the departing spouse or closing escrow agent (the retaining spouse cannot receive cash back).
  • Both spouses have jointly owned the property for at least 12 months prior to the loan application.

This critical distinction allows you to borrow up to 95% or 97% of the home's value and avoids punitive cash-out pricing penalties.
